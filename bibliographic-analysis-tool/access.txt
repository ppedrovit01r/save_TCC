\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ==============================================================================
\PARstart{T}{he} academic publishing ecosystem generates a continuous stream of metadata with every published issue \cite{donthu2021how}. Despite this abundance, editorial boards and independent researchers face technical and infrastructural constraints when attempting to derive operational intelligence from their publication corpora \cite{stumpf1996periodicos, moral2020software}. In scholarly publishing, editorial stewardship often remains purely reactive rather than data-driven \cite{stumpf1996periodicos}; missing or latent indicators regarding institutional endogeny, reviewer concentration, demographic equity, and citation dispersion prevent boards from formulating proactive indexing and internationalization strategies \cite{marziale2014editorial, Nazarovets2025}

In parallel, investigators conducting Systematic Literature Reviews (SLRs) face operational bottlenecks during evidence aggregation \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines, costa2023integrating}. Manual screening and data extraction from thousands of multi-source studies  induces cognitive fatigue and reproducibility drift, often undermining the rigor required by reporting frameworks like PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines, costa2023integrating, page2021prisma}. These challenges are exacerbated by structural deficiencies in bibliographic data acquisition: commercial indices (e.g., Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed) enforce restrictive pagination quotas and deliver structurally disjoint schemas \cite{pranckute2021web, moral2020software}. Furthermore, raw exports frequently suffer from missing Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs), truncated reference strings, non-standard author naming conventions, and disparate character encodings (e.g., UTF-8 with BOM, CP1252, Latin-1) \cite{pranckute2021web}. When ingested into downstream visualization engines without systematic cleaning, this lexicographic and structural noise breaks topological co-citation links, generates isolated orphan nodes in collaboration graphs, and invalidates temporal mapping \cite{moral2020software, minuzzo2026women, small1973co_citation, kessler1963bibliographic}.

To resolve these vulnerabilities without imposing the prohibitive licensing fees, steep operational learning curves, or data lock-in characteristic of commercial suites, this work introduces a unified, open-source, local-execution decision-support platform. Implemented in Python and Streamlit framework \cite{streamlit2025}, the proposed artifact builds upon and substantially expands the exploratory prototype established by Asano et al. (2026) \cite{asano2026simplifying}. Whereas the precursor demonstrated the feasibility of web-based local bibliometric interfaces, it was constrained by synchronous API architectures, a rigid single-format ingestion schema, and elementary descriptive aggregations The present platform advances this foundation into an end-to-end analytical framework by introducing: (i) an agnostic, fault-tolerant multi-format parsing engine; (ii) a deterministic, multi-tier asynchronous enrichment engine querying open knowledge graphs; (iii) dual-algorithm Natural Language Processing ensembles for short-text thematic stratification, coupled with generative LLM labeling; (iv) interactive physics-based topological networks driven by Louvain community detection; and (v) dedicated governance modules for automated institutional endogeny auditing and vector-space SLR screening complaint with Prisma. 

%\PARstart{T}{he} exponential growth of scientific literature has generated an unprecedented influx of bibliographic metadata across open repositories and indexed databases \cite{donthu2021how}. Despite this abundance, academic editorial boards and independent researchers face critical technical barriers when attempting to extract actionable insights from their publishing histories or when conducting Systematic Literature Reviews (SLRs) \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines, moral2020software}.

Section~\ref{sec:methodology} details the core ingestion pipeline, mathematical formulation of scientometric metrics, and algorithmic feature engines. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results and benchmarks applied to both a 10,000-record PubMed benchmark and the historical corpus of the RITA journal. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} addresses architectural trade-offs, practical editorial implications, and threats to validity. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper and outlines future directions. %vai ter discussÃµes?

\section{Related Work and State-of-the-Art Analysis}
\label{sec:related_work}
% ==============================================================================
The theoretical foundation of this work analyzes the prototype used as a base model \cite{asano2026simplifying}, the limitations observed in the state of the art of scientometric tools \cite{moral2020software, cobo2012scimat}, and the physical-mathematical formalization of impact indicators \cite{hirsch2005index, egghe2006improvement, waltman2016review}, network topology \cite{freeman1978centrality, blondel2008fast}, natural language processing \cite{blei2003latent, grootendorst2022bertopic, yin2014dirichlet}, and demographic inference \cite{santamaria2018comparison, holman2018gender}. A review of existing bibliometric software tools revealed key trade-offs, usability barriers, and memory management constraints \cite{donthu2021how, minuzzo2026women, moral2020software}. %acho que nÃ£o precisa citar tanto aqui ainda

\textbf{Asano et al. Prototype (2026):} Asano et al. \cite{asano2026simplifying} developed a lightweight Python and Streamlit prototype aimed at democratizing bibliometric mapping by employing Microsoft Excel (.xlsx) as an intermediate curation canvas. Although validating the viability of interactive Python web frameworks for local desktop analytics, the artifact exhibited critical computational bottlenecks. Ingestion was strictly coupled to a static seven-column schema, requiring external pre-formatting and rejecting other inputs. Furthermore, metadata repair relied on blocking, single-threaded synchronous REST calls to Crossref, requiring upwards of two hours per attribute across corpora of 10,000 records \cite{asano2026simplifying}. The analytical pipeline was limited to classical descriptive aggregates, basic bag-of-words keyword counts via NLTK \cite{bird2009natural},  and unweighted NetworkX graph layouts \cite{hagberg2008exploring}, lacking support for deduplication, demographic attribution, or editorial governance auditing. %bird 2009 nÃ£o estÃ¡ no bib

\textbf{Downstream Visual Renderers (VOSviewer and CiteSpace):} VOSviewer, introduced by van Eck and Waltman \cite{vaneck2010software, waltman2016review}, is widely adopted due to its normalized Visualization of Similarities (VOS) clustering and density rendering algorithms. However, VOSviewer functions strictly as an end-of-pipe network renderer; it possesses no native ingestion correction pipelines, cannot resolve missing metadata attributes, and requires external preprocessing scripts to rectify database-specific encoding discrepancies \cite{moral2020software}. CiteSpace II \cite{chen2006citespace} specializes in temporal visual analytics, utilizing Kleinberg burst detection algorithms and Pathfinder network pruning to trace paradigm shifts and intellectual turning points. Nevertheless, CiteSpace is constrained by a complex desktop interface, high configuration overhead, and numeric instability when processing sparse or smaller specialized datasets \cite{moral2020software}. Crucially, neither tool provides automated mechanisms for data hygiene verification or editorial governance evaluation. %colocar citaÃ§Ã£o para kleinberg

\textbf{Statistical Scripting Environments (Bibliometrix / Biblioshiny):} Aria and Cuccurullo \cite{aria2017bibliometrix} developed Bibliometrix and its Shiny-based GUI (Biblioshiny), delivering a comprehensive array of quantitative scientometric routines in R \cite{moral2020software, truong2023quantitative}. Despite its mathematical breadth, Bibliometrix loads dense co-occurrence and document-attribute matrices uncompressed into client-side R sessions. In medium-to-large bibliographic collections ($>10,000$ publications), this architecture precipitates severe Out-Of-Memory (OOM) browser termination and memory exhaustion. Moreover, its primary parsing workflows remain optimized around Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus formats, offering limited, error-prone support for heterogeneous multi-database merges or raw medical exports such as PubMed NBIB. %precisa da citaÃ§Ã£o sobre OOM

\textbf{Longitudinal Mapping Systems (SciMAT and Sci2 Tool):} SciMAT \cite{cobo2012scimat} established rigorous standards for longitudinal science mapping, introducing strategic Callon diagrams based on centrality and density metrics across sequential temporal windows \cite{callon1983word, cobo2011approach}. While SciMAT incorporates Levenshtein-based entity deduplication \cite{levenshtein1966binary, christen2012data}, it depends on legacy desktop database architectures and requires labor-intensive manual curation cycles \cite{moral2020software}. The Science of Science (Sci2) Tool \cite{sci22009} similarly supports macro-to-micro network analysis across multi-level structures, yet its decoupled workflows and rigid file formatting impose steep technical barriers for non-computational researchers.

\textbf{Proprietary Graph Platforms and Index Access Barriers:} Cloud-native platforms such as Connected Papers \cite{connectedpapers2020} and Litmaps \cite{litmaps2021} offer intuitive, reactive exploration of citation neighborhoods based on the Semantic Scholar and Crossref graphs \cite{ammar2018construction}. However, their underlying citation networks and similarity embeddings are proprietar. They prohibit the ingestion of custom local records, unindexed institutional archives, or regional collections. Compounding these platform constraints, commercial databases maintain rigid download throttles (WoS limits exports to 500 records per operation; Scopus limits exports to 2,000 records) \cite{pranckute2021web, minuzzo2026women}. This commercial and operational fragmentation underscores the critical necessity for a local, format-agnostic, and self-sufficient analytical engine.

\section{Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:tech_design}
% ==============================================================================

The proposed platform is designed as an open-source, local-execution decision-support ecosystem for advanced scientometric analytics, editorial governance, and Systematic Literature Review (SLR) screening. Methodologically, this work follows the Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm for information systems \cite{peffers2007design} to evolve the lightweight Python/Streamlit prototype introduced by Asano et al. \cite{asano2026simplifying}. Whereas the precursor operated as a monolithic script restricted to static Excel inputs and synchronous Crossref queries \cite{asano2026simplifying}, the present framework re-engineers the software infrastructure under Dijkstra's principle of Separation of Concerns (SoC) \cite{dijkstra1982selected} and loose coupling. This decoupling isolates computationally intensive natural language processing (NLP), graph theory topology, and demographic inference pipelines from reactive UI rendering, eliminating memory leaks and client concurrency bottlenecks during local execution. %tÃ¡ meio repetitivo mas nÃ£o Ã© exatamente a mesma coisa

\subsection{Project Objectives}
\label{subsec:objectives}
Driven by the friction in editorial processes and SLRs, the development of this system is guided by four fundamental design principles: \textit{data sovereignty} (local, offline execution without proprietary lock-in), \textit{format agnosticism} (minimizing fragmentation of commercial schemas), \textit{analytical depth} (transcending basic frequency tallies through topological and semantic mining), and \textit{operational accessibility} (mitigating deployment barriers for non-technical users). %nÃ£o to gostando do terceiro

To realize these principles, the project addresses five engineering and research objectives: 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ensure Universal Bibliographic Interoperability:} Establish a resilient, format-agnostic data normalization pipeline capable of resolving syntactic, encoding, and deduplication inconsistencies across disparate indexing schemas without data loss.
    
    \item \textbf{Accelerate Metadata Completeness via Open Knowledge Graphs:} Overcome rate-limiting bottlenecks and missing citation linkages by establishing an asynchronous, multi-tier harvesting architecture across federated open-access APIs.
    
    \item \textbf{Learn Unsupervised Semantic-Relational Representations:} Leveraging structural graph representations and short-text topic embeddings to bridge bibliometric metrics with unsupervised community detection and domain stratification.
    
    \item \textbf{Automate Governance Auditing and Methodological Traceability:} Formulate quantitative diagnostic modules that evaluate institutional endogeneity, track demographic representation trajectories (e.g., author scissors effect), and enforce PRISMA-compliant systematic screening workflows.
    
    \item \textbf{Empirically Evaluate Analytical Efficacy and System Scalability:} Assess the platform's computational throughput, diagnostic accuracy, and decision-support capability via a real-world case study on an indexed journal publishing archive (e.g., \textit{Revista de InformÃ¡tica TeÃ³rica e Aplicada} -- RITA).

\subsection{Architectural Overview}
\label{subsec:arch_overview}
%As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, the system is organized into five decoupled operational tiers supported by a cross-cutting foundational layer, structured to separate immediate in-memory metrics from heavy on-demand computations:

\begin{figure*}

At its core, the platform operates as a state-driven pipeline divided into two primary phases: Data Preparation (encompassing ingestion, schema homogenization, deduplication, and metadata enrichment) and the Analytical Workspace (hosting specialized scientometric, thematic, and governance engines). To ensure frictionless deployment across diverse operating systems without requiring external database setups or containerization skills, all runtime libraries, NLP tokenizers, and offline web assets are packaged via a declarative \texttt{requirements.txt} manifest, allowing end users to initialize the complete analytical environment with a single package manager invocation.

Computational data lives securely in project-based directory sandboxes managed by local SQLite request caches and asynchronous run loggers, guaranteeing reproducibility and strict local data sovereignty. Within the reactive runtime \cite{streamlit2025}, state management enforces a pointer duality between the global corpus (\texttt{master\_df}) and an active analytical slice (\texttt{working\_df}), allowing downstream exploratory modules to operate on filtered sub-graphs in ``Global Focus Mode'' without mutating the underlying validated baseline.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, data transitions sequentially from heterogeneous raw exports through a canonical 24-field normalization layer and a deterministic multi-tier API cascade (federating OpenAlex \cite{priem2022openalex}, Crossref, PubMed, and Semantic Scholar \cite{ammar2018construction}) before being exposed to decoupled analytical modules. These specialized feature engines compute metrics on demand and feed into a universal reverse-serialization layer, persisting fully enriched bibliographies back into standard academic formats \cite{aria2017bibliometrix, vaneck2010software}.

\subsection{Core Pipeline: Ingestion, Deduplication, and Enrichment}
\label{subsec:core_engine}

The core processing pipeline (\texttt{core/}) establishes an automated, fault-tolerant workflow that transforms unstructured, differently formatted bibliographic corpora into a dense, verified dataset ready for scientometric modeling and network extraction\cite{donthu2021how, moral2020software}.

\subsubsection{Data Ingestion and Schema Normalization (\texttt{ingestion.py})}
The ingestion engine acts as the front-line abstraction layer against structural and syntactic variance across academic repositories such as Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed \cite{pranckute2021web, waltman2016review}. Rather than executing rigid tabular ingestion, the module executes an \textit{Intelligent Sniffing State Machine} \cite{asano2026simplifying, mckinney2010pandas}. When evaluating tabular inputs (\texttt{.csv} or \texttt{.xlsx}), the parser sequentially validates candidate character encodings (\texttt{utf-8-sig}, \texttt{latin1}, \texttt{cp1252}) against common delimiters (\texttt{','} and \texttt{';'}) to instantiate an uncorrupted \texttt{pandas.DataFrame} without terminating the runtime upon malformed byte sequences \cite{mckinney2010pandas}. For structured citation streams, the system interfaces with \texttt{rispy} (for \texttt{.ris} files) and \texttt{bibtexparser} (for \texttt{.bib} files). Unstructured PubMed exports (\texttt{.nbib}) are processed through a custom finite state machine (\texttt{parse\_nbib}) that iteratively tracks proprietary National Library of Medicine (NLM) field tags, stitches multi-line text continuations, and extracts metadata attributes via compiled regular expressions.

Following raw loading, the parsed schema is normalized against a canonical 24-column taxonomy via a static dictionary that harmonizes heterogeneous column conventions across platforms (e.g., \textit{pmid}, \textit{pubmed id}, and \textit{pmc}) \cite{asano2026simplifying}. To counteract sparse metadata prior to network operations, the module executes \textit{Cross-Field Deduction Heuristics}: when geographic metadata such as author \texttt{Country} is null, compiled regular expressions scan unstructured \texttt{Address} or \texttt{Affiliations} attributes for localized administrative sub-divisions (e.g., Brazilian state abbreviations such as ``RS'' or ``SP'') and international municipal entities, programmatically inferring demographic attributes prior to downstream analysis.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Record Consolidation (\texttt{deduplication.py})}
To consolidate duplicate records across multi-database queries while handling minor lexicographical deviations, the platform abandons naive deduplication in favor of a multi-stage deterministic pipeline rooted in entity resolution principles \cite{christen2012data, navarro2001guided, levenshtein1966binary}. The algorithm first applies aggressive canonical normalization to persistent identifiers: Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) are stripped of URI schemes (e.g., \texttt{https://doi.org/}) and lowercased; OpenAlex identifiers \cite{priem2022openalex} are isolated and converted to uppercase; and PubMed IDs (PMIDs) are validated against numeric regular expressions. Identity matching executes sequentially across priority tiers: exact DOI match, followed by OpenAlex ID, and PMID. As an empirical fallback for unindexed entries, the algorithm performs normalized title plus publication year matching. Titles are stripped of bracketed translation notations (e.g., \texttt{[Title...]}) and punctuation, and verified under an exact-match constraint with a length boundary ($L > 10$ characters), paired with the publication year to securely eliminate false positives on generic single-word headers (e.g., textbook titles overriding specific papers) \cite{navarro2001guided}.

To preserve maximum information density during record merging, consolidation follows an objective completeness heuristic[cite: 3]. For each candidate row $i$, a richness score is calculated:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:richness_heuristic}
\text{Richness}_i = \left( N_{\text{non-null}, i} \times 10^6 \right) + C_i
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{non-null}, i}$ represents the count of non-null attributes in row $i$ and $C_i$ denotes its reported citation count (\texttt{TimesCited})[cite: 3]. When duplicates are grouped, records are sorted by $\text{Richness}_i$ in descending order; the row maximizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:richness_heuristic} is selected as the primary surviving instance. Any sparse, unpopulated fields in the survivor row are then horizontally backward-filled (\texttt{bfill(axis=1)}) using valid values present across the discarded duplicate rows, guaranteeing a lossless, maximally complete output record.

\subsubsection{Multi-Tier API Enrichment Engine (\texttt{enrichment.py})}
The enrichment engine resolves metadata incompleteness (specifically retrieving missing abstracts, publication years, and Open Access statuses) without triggering HTTP 429 (\textit{Too Many Requests}) rate-limit terminations. As illustrated in the deterministic fallback cascade, network requests traverse five sequential tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (OpenAlex Batch):} Batches un-enriched DOIs in chunks of 50 records and queries the OpenAlex REST API under a strict rate limiter ($\approx 6.6$ requests/second) in compliance with the service's Polite Pool policies.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Crossref REST):} Dispatches targeted REST calls to Crossref for records unresolved by OpenAlex.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (NCBI eUtils):} Queries the NCBI \texttt{eFetch} endpoint for un-enriched PMIDs, strictly enforcing a 0.35-second inter-request delay to honor NCBI's threshold of 3 requests/second, parsing nested XML nodes via \texttt{xml.etree.ElementTree}.
    \item \textbf{Tier 4 (Semantic Scholar Graph API):} Queries academic graph endpoints using batched POST payloads.
    \item \textbf{Tier 5 (Fuzzy Title Disambiguation):} Issues final title searches on OpenAlex via \texttt{difflib.SequenceMatcher} \cite{navarro2001guided}, enforcing an exact similarity cutoff ($\ge 82\%$) to prevent false positive entity hijacking.
\end{itemize}

To ensure robustness and state preservation across these layers, the pipeline operates asynchronously through an explicitly defined state machine. The following code snippet demonstrates the execution of the fallback cascade mapping arrays and missing fields:

\begin{verbatim}
def apply_openalex_data(oa_data: dict, row: pd.Series, fields_to_enrich: list,
                        updates: dict, missed: list, is_ultimate: bool = False):
    needs_update = lambda f: f in fields_to_enrich and \
                             (_get_field(row, f) is None or is_ultimate)

    # 1. Author Name Parsing and Disambiguation
    if needs_update('Author') and oa_data.get('authorships'):
        updates['Author'] = format_authors(oa_data['authorships'])
        
    # 2. Abstract Extraction via Inverted Index
    if needs_update('Abstract') and oa_data.get('abstract_inverted_index'):
        updates['Abstract'] = reconstruct_abstract(oa_data['abstract_inverted_index'])

    # 3. Reference Topology Edge Linking
    if needs_update('Article References') and oa_data.get('referenced_works'):
        updates['Article References'] = format_references(oa_data['referenced_works'])
        
    # Track any fields that remained unresolved
    for field in fields_to_enrich:
        if field not in updates and needs_update(field):
            missed.append(field)
\end{verbatim}

Network resilience is guaranteed by intercepting all outgoing calls through \texttt{requests\_cache}, persisting HTTP transaction payloads into an isolated, local SQLite database with a 7-day time-to-live (TTL) cache. OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar responses are additionally persisted using on-disk JSON registries. This architecture provides persistent idempotency: in the event of an unexpected thread failure or process termination, execution resumes instantly from the exact row where it was interrupted without re-issuing completed network calls.

\subsubsection{Universal Reverse Serialization (\texttt{export.py})}
To avoid trapping enriched outputs inside the platform runtime, \texttt{core/export.py} translates enriched in-memory \texttt{DataFrames} back into standard bibliometric interchange syntaxes via dedicated generators (\texttt{df\_to\_ris}, \texttt{df\_to\_bib}, \texttt{df\_to\_nbib}, and \texttt{df\_to\_csv}). Before byte serialization, the module runs a cleansing routine (\texttt{\_clean\_df()}) that iterates through DataFrame columns, recursively detects nested objects or arrays produced by downstream NLP and entity-tagging modules, and serializes them into delimited scalar strings. Accidental duplicate column headers are consolidated via horizontal backward filling. The resulting string payload is encoded to byte-level streams using the \texttt{utf-8-sig} encoding schemeâ€”ensuring character and diacritic preservation across spreadsheet environments such as Microsoft Excelâ€”and registered in transient cache memory blocks (\texttt{st.cache\_data}) for direct client-side download.

\subsection{Data Presentation \& Analytics (\texttt{dashboard/})}

Guided by Dijkstra's separation of concerns \cite{dijkstra1982selected}, this subsystem isolates analytical and mathematical pipelines from visual rendering components to circumvent memory overhead and browser-freezing bottlenecks common in reactive scientometric interfaces \cite{asano2026simplifying, moral2020software}.

\subsubsection{Bibliometric Indices and Inequality Mathematics (\texttt{dashboard/metrics\_impact.py})}

Author-level impact evaluation requires non-trivial aggregations across multi-author publications. Rather than relying on iterative row scans such as Pandas \texttt{iterrows()}, which suffer from substantial interpreter overhead and scale poorly on medium-to-large corpora \cite{asano2026simplifying, mckinney2010pandas}, the \texttt{metrics\_impact.py} module implements a fully vectorized aggregation pipeline via \texttt{\_compute\_metrics\_per\_author\_vectorized}. Sanitization executes an aggressive filtering stage that purges ambiguous and invalid tokens (\texttt{'et al'}, \texttt{'nan'}, \texttt{'anonymous'}) from author lists. Subsequently, columnar grouping operations (\texttt{groupby("Author").agg()}) powered by optimized NumPy arrays \cite{harris2020numpy} compute total and mean citation counts instantaneously across the active publication dataset.

To compute non-linear bibliometric indicators without recurring algorithmic bottlenecks, the module isolates the citation arrays of authors with non-zero citations, sorts them in strictly descending order ($C_1 \ge C_2 \ge \dots \ge C_n$), and applies optimized generator comprehensions. For the classical Hirsch index ($h$-index) \cite{hirsch2005index}, the algorithm determines the maximum integer $h$ by evaluating:
\begin{equation}
h = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{I}(C_{i+1} \ge i + 1)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}$ represents the indicator function, mapped in the implementation via \texttt{sum(c >= i + 1)}.

For the $g$-index \cite{egghe2006}, introduced to compensate for the insensitivity of the $h$-index to highly cited outlier publications (``blockbusters'') \cite{waltman2016review}, the algorithm requires identifying the highest rank $g$ such that the cumulative sum of citations of the top $g$ articles satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{g} C_i \ge g^2
\end{equation}
The engine tracks this prefix sum iteratively within the generator, breaking once the cumulative condition $\sum C_i \ge i^2$ is violated.

Beyond point indices, the module quantifies the systemic distribution of scientific impact across the collection \cite{albarran2011skewness} by modeling citation inequality. In classical scientometrics, the Gini coefficient ($G$) is formulated through double pairwise relative differences \cite{gini1912, lorenz1905}:
\begin{equation}
G = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_i - x_j|}{2n^2\overline{x}}
\end{equation}
Because computing this theoretical formulation directly incurs an $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ time complexity that degrades performance on large datasets, \texttt{metrics\_impact.py} implements an equivalent discrete, linear-time cumulative formulation operating over the sorted citation vector ($C_{(1)} \le C_{(2)} \le \dots \le C_{(n)}$):
\begin{equation}
G = \frac{n + 1 - 2 \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} S_i}{S_n} \right)}{n}
\end{equation}
where $S_i = \sum_{k=1}^i C_{(k)}$ is the running prefix sum (\texttt{cumsum}) up to the $i$-th publication, and $S_n = \text{total\_citations}$ represents the aggregate volume of citations. Concurrently, the module dynamically renders the associated Lorenz curve, plotting the cumulative proportion of publications against the cumulative proportion of citations to give researchers an immediate graphical and mathematical diagnostic of citation concentration \cite{albarran2011skewness}.

Annual obsolescence and temporal decay are derived from moving averages:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:avg_citations}
\bar{C}_Y =
\begin{cases}
\frac{C_Y}{P_Y}, & \text{if } P_Y > 0 \
\text{NaN}, & \text{if } P_Y = 0
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
protected via numeric exception trapping to maintain continuous temporal curves \cite{moed2010measuring}.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Workspace Routing and Focused Exploration (\texttt{dashboard/main.py} \& \texttt{articles\_explorer.py})}

The modular architecture coordinates navigation through \texttt{dashboard/main.py}, which functions as the unified state router across Phase 2 of the platform. Under Streamlit's reactive execution cycle, user interactions trigger full script reruns. To prevent re-reading storage or repeatedly re-instantiating heavy Pandas \texttt{DataFrame} objects in system RAM \cite{asano2026, mckinney2010}, \texttt{dashboard/main.py} reads the central dataset from \texttt{st.session\_state.master\_df} and binds it into a persistent sidebar navigation harness.

The \texttt{articles\_explorer.py} module consumes this shared global state to deliver an interactive querying engine. Operating on the active matrix, it constructs multi-dimensional boolean bitmasks in Pandas across temporal intervals ($[Y_{\min}, Y_{\max}]$), substring author matches, and publication venues.

Crucially, \texttt{articles\_explorer.py} serves as the gateway to the platform's ``Global Focus Mode''. When an investigator isolates a specific bibliometric cohort, the module overwrites \texttt{st.session\_state.working\_df} with the filtered subset. This truncated data structure is automatically propagated across all downstream computational modulesâ€”including thematic stratification, topological network extraction (\texttt{network\_topology.py}) based on Louvain community clustering \cite{blondel2008}, and latent semantic processing (\texttt{thematic\_stratification.py}) via BERTopic and GSDMM \cite{grootendorst2022, yin2014, piceni2025}. Consequently, downstream tools compute exclusively on the dynamically bounded sub-graph without requiring manual dataset reloads or data duplication.








\subsection{Thematic Stratification (\texttt{features/thematic\_stratification.py})}
To address the severe lexical sparsity and high semantic density characteristic of short scientific abstracts \cite{qiang2019short, albalawi2020using, amorim2022topic}, this module implements a dual-algorithm Natural Language Processing (NLP) ensemble. Conventional clustering algorithms often degrade when applied to short texts due to limited term co-occurrence. The module mitigates this by coupling a dense semantic pipeline with a probabilistic bag-of-words framework.

The semantic pipeline executes a BERTopic architecture \cite{grootendorst2022bertopic}: input documents are mapped into a high-dimensional vector space using pre-trained Transformer embeddings, capturing latent contextual semantics. To alleviate the curse of dimensionality, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) \cite{mcinnes2018umap} performs non-linear dimensionality reduction while preserving both local and global topological structure. Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) \cite{mcinnes2017hdbscan} then identifies dense clusters across arbitrary geometric shapes. Cluster-level keyword representations are subsequently extracted via class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF).

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:c_tf_idf}
W_{t, c} = \text{TF}{t, c} \times \log\left(1 + \frac{A}{f_t}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\text{TF}{t, c}$ represents the frequency of term $t$ in cluster $c$, $f_t$ denotes the aggregate frequency of term $t$ across all clusters, and $A$ denotes the average number of words per cluster.

In parallel, the module deploys Gibbs Sampling Dirichlet Multinomial Mixture (GSDMM) \cite{yin2014dirichlet}, a probabilistic topic model optimized for short texts. Unlike Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) \cite{blei2003latent, blei2012probabilistic}, which assumes each document is an arbitrary mixture of multiple topics, GSDMM operates under the one-topic-per-document assumption. Cluster selection is modeled via a Dirichlet prior and word emission via multinomial sampling according to:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gsdmm_formula}
P(z_d=k \mid \vec{d}, \vec{z}{\neg d}) \propto \frac{m{k, \neg d} + \alpha}{D - 1 + K\alpha} \times \frac{\prod_{w \in d} (n_{k,w, \neg d} + \beta)}{\prod_{i=1}^{|d|} (N_{k, \neg d} + V\beta + i - 1)}
\end{equation}
where $m_{k, \neg d}$ is the count of documents assigned to topic $k$ excluding document $d$; $D$ is the total document volume; $K$ is the designated maximum topic count; $n_{k,w, \neg d}$ is the occurrence count of word $w$ in cluster $k$ excluding occurrences in $d$; $N_{k, \neg d}$ is the total word count in cluster $k$ excluding $d$; $V$ is vocabulary size; and $\alpha, \beta$ are Dirichlet symmetric smoothing priors governing cluster dispersion and word affinity, respectively \cite{yin2014dirichlet}.

To evaluate topic coherence and cluster agreement, the pipeline calculates Normalized Pointwise Mutual Information (NPMI) \cite{roeder2015exploring} across top lexical terms:\begin{equation}\label{eq:npmi}\text{NPMI}(w_i, w_j) = \frac{\log \frac{P(w_i, w_j)}{P(w_i)P(w_j)}}{-\log P(w_i, w_j)},\end{equation}while Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO) \cite{webber2010similarity} evaluates ranking similarity between topic vocabularies:\begin{equation}\label{eq:rbo}\text{RBO} = (1 - p) \sum_{d=1}^{d_{\text{max}}} p^{d-1} A_d\end{equation}where $p$ is a persistence parameter ($p=0.9$) and $A_d$ is the agreement proportion at depth $d$.To reconcile the dense spatial embeddings from BERTopic with the probabilistic lexical micro-clusters from GSDMM, the platform implements a \textbf{Consensus-First Many-to-One Alignment Engine}. Rather than isolating models, the system maps every GSDMM cluster to its closest BERTopic leader pillar using a hybrid similarity metric ($60\%$ document co-occurrence Jaccard $+ 40\%$ keyword vocabulary overlap).

The pipeline executes a \textbf{Two-Pass Iterative Refinement}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pass 1 (Consensus Core Identification):} Documents where both BERTopic and GSDMM agree receive a strict consensus flag and $100\%$ confidence. These pure consensus cores calculate high-purity topic centroids in the embedding space ($\mathbf{c}_k = \frac{\sum_{i \in \text{Core}_k} \mathbf{e}_i}{\|\sum_{i \in \text{Core}_k} \mathbf{e}_i\|}$).
    \item \textbf{Pass 2 (Centroid Boundary Resolution):} Boundary and divergent documents are projected against all topic centroids via cosine similarity. If a document aligns strongly with an alternative topic's centroid, it is decisively reassigned with calibrated confidence. 
\end{enumerate}
This architecture completely eliminates empty "ghost topics" and guarantees robust, well-populated macro-thematic pillars. Finally, topics are mapped onto a strategic Callon Diagram \cite{callon1983word, cobo2011approach, cobo2012scimat}, positioning clusters along density and centrality coordinates derived from the co-word equivalence index $e_{ij}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:callon_metrics}
e_{ij} = \frac{(c_{ij})^2}{c_i \times c_j}, \quad C_k = 10 \sum_{i \in k, j \notin k} e_{ij}, \quad D_k = \frac{100}{N_k} \sum_{i,j \in k} e_{ij}
\end{equation}
stratifying topics into motor themes (high centrality, high density), highly developed/isolated themes, emerging/declining themes, and basic/transversal themes \cite{cobo2011approach}.

\subsection{Thematic Flow Mapping (\texttt{features/thematic\_flow.py})}
The longitudinal evolution of scientific disciplines is tracked through temporal topic modeling across user-parameterized chronological windows (eras) \cite{cobo2011approach, lemos2024}. Abstracts are partitioned according to publication year, establishing discrete temporal sub-corpora. Within each temporal window, the sub-corpus is vectorized into a term-frequency--inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) matrix and decomposed using Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) \cite{lee1999learning}. NMF decomposes the document-term matrix $V$ into non-negative low-rank factor matrices $W$ and $H$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nmf}
\min_{W \ge 0, H \ge 0} \frac{1}{2} |V - W H|F^2, \quad K = \max\left(3, ; \min\left(12, ; \lfloor N{\text{era}} / 35 \rfloor\right)\right),
\end{equation}
yielding additive, parts-based semantic representations with substantially lower computational overhead than transformer vectorization over small temporal slices.To model conceptual continuity, merging, and divergence across successive eras, the module constructs a directed acyclic graph (DAG) \cite{cobo2011approach}. Topical continuity between topic $A$ in era $T$ and topic $B$ in era $T+1$ is established by computing the Jaccard similarity coefficient \cite{jaccard1901etude} between their top $k$ representative lexical features:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jaccard_sankey}
J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \quad \text{Flow} = \max\left(1, ; \lfloor J(A, B) \times N_{\text{docs}}(T_{t, i}) \times 10 \rfloor\right).
\end{equation}

When $J(A, B)$ satisfies or exceeds an empirical threshold, a directed edge is assigned between the nodes, weighted by the flow magnitude. These nodes and edges are mapped into tabular arrays and rendered as an interactive Sankey diagram using Plotly \cite{plotly2015}, visually documenting paradigm shifts, conceptual splits, and intellectual consolidation across decades.

\subsection{Network Topology and Centrality (\texttt{features/network\_topology.py})}
This module translates tabular bibliographic registries into adjacency matrices for structural and topological graph analysis using NetworkX \cite{hagberg2008exploring}. It constructs two foundational scientometric graph typologies:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Co-citation Networks:} In which an undirected edge between two cited references represents the frequency with which both appear together in the bibliography of citing documents \cite{small1973co_citation}.
\item \textbf{Bibliographic Coupling Networks:} In which an undirected edge between two published papers indicates the number of shared bibliographic references cited by both works \cite{kessler1963bibliographic}.
\end{enumerate}

To identify latent scientific communities and disciplinary clusters, the module applies community detection through the Louvain algorithm \cite{blondel2008fast}, maximizing Newman-Girvan modularity ($Q$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:louvain}
Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ is edge weight between nodes $i$ and $j$, $k_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$ is the sum of weights attached to node $i$, $m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij} A_{ij}$, and $\delta(c_i, c_j)$ is 1 if nodes $i$ and $j$ belong to the same community, and 0 otherwise.

Concurrently, the module computes a vector of centrality metrics to determine nodal prestige and structural brokerage:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Degree Centrality:} Quantifies direct connectivity and local visibility \cite{freeman1978centrality}.
\item \textbf{Betweenness Centrality:} Measures the ratio of shortest paths traversing a given node via Brandes' algorithm \cite{brandes2001faster}, identifying gatekeepers and interdisciplinary bridges.
\item \textbf{Closeness Centrality:} Assesses the mean geodesic distance from a node to all other reachable nodes \cite{freeman1978centrality}.
\item \textbf{PageRank and Eigenvector Centrality:} Quantifies prestige by weighting connections according to the centrality of adjacent neighbors \cite{page1999pagerank}. Numerical instability in disconnected subgraphs is mitigated via explicit error handling for power-iteration non-convergence.
\end{itemize}

To ensure real-time responsiveness and eliminate browser-freezing bottlenecks, the platform implements an inverted index architecture for Bibliographic Coupling. Rather than executing an $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ pairwise loop across millions of reference string comparisons, the system maps each reference directly to its citing articles in $\mathcal{O}(N)$ time. This optimization reduces coupling computations from several minutes to under four seconds for a 10,000-article corpus. Graph layouts are generated through force-directed algorithms \cite{fruchterman1991graph}, serialized into PyVis objects using remote CDN resources (ensuring UTF-8 encoding safety), and embedded securely within Streamlit via isolated HTML iframes.

\subsection{Global Demographics and Transnational Networks}
The demographics module evaluates spatial publication distribution and cross-border scientific cooperation. To overcome frequent geographic attribution errors---such as the ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 code collision where the common Portuguese/Spanish preposition "de" (as in \textit{Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais}) is falsely tagged as Germany (\texttt{DE})---the platform enforces strict regex word boundaries (\verb|\b|) and excludes linguistic stopwords. Institutional strings are parsed to populate an Equal Earth projected choropleth map. Transnational cooperative weight is modeled as a mathematical graph where edges represent co-authorships between distinct extracted nations.

\subsection{Demographic Disambiguation and Equity Auditing (\texttt{features/gender.py})}
Designed for scientometric equity auditing, this module evaluates demographic representation and institutional hierarchy within authorship lists, focusing on the empirical quantification of the ``Scissors Effect'': the progressive attrition of female researchers from lead-author (mentee) positions to senior or last-author (principal investigator) roles \cite{holman2018gender, minuzzo2026women, campbell2013gender, yang2022gender}.

The module executes a deterministic string-parsing pipeline capable of standardizing disparate citation syntaxes, such as Anglo-Saxon inverted formats (\texttt{Surname, Forename}) and medical index abbreviations (\texttt{Surname Initial}). To support non-Latin character sets, strings undergo Unicode normalization and phonetic transliteration via \texttt{unidecode}.

To address name-to-gender classification constraints and prevent API rate-limiting, the system implements a multi-tiered disambiguation architecture:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Author Entity Resolution:} Unexpanded initials are resolved against the OpenAlex Knowledge Graph API \cite{priem2022openalex} using co-authorship and affiliation metadata to recover canonical full names.
\item \textbf{Persistent Local Storage:} Disambiguated names are queried against an indexed on-disk JSON dictionary (\texttt{gender_cache.json}).
\item \textbf{Batched Remote Ingestion:} Unresolved records are batched and transmitted to external classification services (e.g., NamSor \cite{santamaria2018comparison}), with predictions asynchronously appended to the local disk cache.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Systematic Review State Machine (\texttt{features/prisma\_assistant.py})}
To support the methodological execution of SLRs under the PRISMA 2020 statement \cite{page2021prisma} and Kitchenham's guidelines for Software Engineering \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines, petersen2008systematic, petersen2015guidelines}, this module instantiates an explicit state machine inside \texttt{st.session_state} \cite{costa2023integrating}.

The state engine formalizes screening into four deterministic phases: Identification, Screening, Eligibility, and Inclusion. A pristine version of the imported dataset (post-deduplication, pre-screening) is securely backed up in the session state (\texttt{raw\_df\_backup}). This ensures that downstream thematic and network modules maintain structural integrity and accurate total counts without being warped by PRISMA exclusion criteria. The flow renders natively via Streamlit's Graphviz component, generating a fully compliant PRISMA 2020 DOT flowchart that accurately computes differences between imported records, deduplicated sets, and categorized exclusions. The flow renders as an interactive SVG diagram and exports reproducible \texttt{.dot} and vector source files for publication.

\subsection{Institutional Endogeneity and Governance (\texttt{features/demographics.py})}
This module serves as an automated editorial compliance audit by computing the Institutional Endogeneity Index ($E_{\text{inst}}$) \cite{marziale2014editorial, Nazarovets2025, pinto2024endogamia, stumpf1996periodicos}. In scientometrics, institutional endogeneity measures the proportion of published articles authored by individuals affiliated with the journal's publishing institution. High endogeneity undermines editorial impartiality and serves as an explicit disqualification criterion in major indexing systems. In particular, the official SciELO guidelines (\textit{Critérios, políticas e procedimentos para a admissão e a permanência de periódicos na Coleção SciELO Brasil}, Section~5.3.2: \textit{Avaliação de formato e endogenia}) establish institutional and geographic concentration among authors and editorial actors as an explicit limiting factor (\textit{fator limitante}) for journal indexing and retention \cite{scielo2020criterios}.

To audit compliance, the algorithm normalizes affiliation strings across the dataset and performs a vectorized boolean scan against the host institutional identifier ($I_{\text{pub}}$) and its disambiguated aliases:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:endogenia}
E_{\text{inst}} = \left( \frac{\sum_{a=1}^{A} \mathbb{I}(I_a \cap I_{\text{pub}} \neq \emptyset)}{A} \right) \times 100
\end{equation}
where $A$ denotes the total number of evaluated articles with valid affiliation records, and $I_a = \{\text{aff}_{a,1}, \dots, \text{aff}_{a,m}\}$ represents the set of all institutional affiliations declared across all co-authors of article $a$. The indicator function $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ evaluates to $1$ if \textit{any} author is affiliated with the publishing institution $I_{\text{pub}}$ (or its user-selected aliases), and $0$ otherwise. Counting articles containing any internal co-author establishes a strict, conservative audit upper bound, mitigating false-negative risks and accounting for the fact that heterogeneous multi-database exports do not systematically isolate reprint or corresponding author affiliations.

The safety margin relative to the tolerance threshold is dynamically monitored via:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margem_ref}
\Delta_{\text{ref}} = \text{Threshold}_{\text{ref}} - E_{\text{inst}}
\end{equation}
While SciELO qualitatively defines institutional concentration as a limiting factor, Brazilian scientometric practice and CAPES/Qualis evaluation committees empirically adopt an upper tolerance benchmark of $\text{Threshold}_{\text{ref}} = 20\%$ for affiliated authors \cite{marziale2014editorial, pinto2024endogamia, stumpf1996periodicos}. The module implements this $20\%$ benchmark as a default reference while exposing an interactive configuration slider ($0\% \le \text{Threshold}_{\text{ref}} \le 100\%$) in the user interface, enabling editorial teams to calibrate thresholds according to domain-specific indexing mandates. If endogeneity exceeds this reference bound ($\Delta_{\text{ref}} < 0$), the module dispatches automated diagnostic warnings within the UI, highlighting specific volume outliers and editorial compliance risks \cite{marziale2014editorial, Nazarovets2025}.

\subsection{Data Compliance and Caching Architecture (\texttt{utils/cache/})}
\label{subsec:cache_compliance}

To optimize performance and mitigate rate-limiting against external services (e.g., NamSor, OpenAlex, and translation engines) \cite{priem2022openalex, santamaria2018comparison}, the platform implements a deterministic caching architecture. Operating strictly as an \textit{API Quota Preserver}, the system utilizes flat key-value JSON stores to suppress redundant network calls. For example, \texttt{gender\_cache.json} maps normalized given names to inferred gender probabilities, executing a billable API transaction only upon a cache miss. Similarly, \texttt{translation\_cache.json} maps an MD5 \rev{hash (used as a deterministic content key, not for cryptographic security)} of foreign-language abstracts to their English counterparts, while \texttt{openalex\_works\_cache.json} caches \rev{public-domain} academic metadata.

\begin{revbblock}
From a regulatory compliance perspective, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Brazilian General Data Protection Law (LGPD), and third-party API Terms of Service, the caches were designed to minimize the retention of personal data. The system does not store raw institutional credentials, harvest private analytics, or circumvent anti-scraping policies. The gender cache stores inferred probabilities keyed by isolated given names rather than by fully identified individuals, and the OpenAlex and ORCID caches store scholarly metadata released under the Creative Commons CC0 1.0 Universal Public Domain Dedication. Nevertheless, inferred gender attributes linked to identifiable author lists may constitute personal data under the GDPR and LGPD, the redistribution of third-party inference results may be restricted by commercial API service agreements (e.g., NamSor), and translated abstracts may remain subject to publisher copyright. Accordingly, users are advised to evaluate these legal and contractual constraints before distributing cache files in public repositories. In the event of cache eviction or manual deletion, the platform degrades gracefully, reconstructing required metadata on-demand via standard fallback API queries.
\end{revbblock}

The \texttt{lib/} directory encapsulates external JavaScript/HTML client bindings and the core mathematical engines that drive computation independently of the Streamlit presentation layer.

\subsubsection{Client-Side Interface Bindings}

    \item \textbf{Advanced Selection (}\texttt{tom-select/}\textbf{):} A lightweight JavaScript control framework that powers responsive multi-select input dropdowns across the application's parameter configuration panels.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Topic Modeling Engine (\texttt{lib/topic\_modeling/})}
The computational core of thematic stratification is partitioned from UI execution logic across modular, algorithmic components.

\paragraph{Pipeline Orchestration (\texttt{ensemble.py})}
Acts as the central coordination layer for the dual-model NLP architecture. Prior to loading dense neural representations, the orchestrator suppresses low-level C++ TensorFlow logging and HuggingFace Hub telemetry. 

The module coordinates specialized preprocessing via \texttt{preprocess\_for\_gsdmm} and \texttt{run\_bertopic}. It implements a domain-specific stopword filter designed for bibliometrics: beyond conventional natural language stopwords (e.g., NLTK dictionaries), it strips academic boilerplate tokens (e.g., \textit{``paper''}, \textit{``results''}, \textit{``analysis''}) and traps common HTTP web-scraping artifacts that degrade bibliographic corpora (e.g., \textit{``error1500thats''}, \textit{``forbidden''}, \textit{``bad gateway''}). The refined token matrix is subsequently vectorized via \texttt{CountVectorizer} and dispatched to the BERTopic and GSDMM execution pipelines.

\paragraph{Probabilistic Short-Text Modeling (\texttt{gsdmm\_mgp.py})}
Because geometric density estimators like HDBSCAN (utilized within standard BERTopic architectures) frequently degrade on sparse, short-text feature spaces, this module implements a standalone Gibbs Sampling Dirichlet Multinomial Mixture (GSDMM) algorithm based on the Movie Group Process (MGP) formalized by Yin and Wang (2014).

Rather than evaluating spatial geometric distances in latent space, GSDMM calculates cluster allocation probabilistically under the metaphor of a clustering process:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A corpus of documents (students) is arbitrarily assigned across $K$ initial clusters (tables).
    \item Over $N$ Markov chain iterations, a document $d$ is removed from its current cluster and reassigned based on a conditional probability score.
    \item Cluster selection is governed by two hyperparameters:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\alpha$: Controls the probability of an unassigned document occupying an empty cluster, tuning overall cluster dispersion and fragmentation.
        \item $\beta$: Dictates the affinity of a document toward clusters demonstrating high lexical co-occurrence with its own token set.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

To maintain numerical stability when multiplying small probabilities across extensive vocabularies, the module computes a log-likelihood scoring function:
\begin{equation}
    p(z_d = k \mid \vec{z}_{-d}, \vec{d}) = \exp(lN_1 - lD_1 + lN_2 - lD_2)
\end{equation}
where $lN$ and $lD$ represent the respective logarithmic numerator and denominator components of the prior and conditional lexical probabilities. Document migration iterates until the cluster transition frequency stabilizes below an empirical convergence threshold, yielding tightly bounded micro-clusters optimized for short bibliographic abstracts.

\paragraph{Semantic Labeling Pipeline (\texttt{genai.py})}
Bridges the mathematical cluster outputs with generative large language models (LLMs) to produce human-interpretable taxonomies. The module aggregates top-ranked lexical keyword vectors from both BERTopic and GSDMM, partitions them into batches of 25 clusters to conform to contextual token windows, and submits structured JSON-schema prompts to target execution endpoints (e.g., Google Gemini, Groq/LLaMA 3, or local Ollama instances). The pipeline enforces a deterministic schema returning mappings of the form:
\begin{center}
    $\langle \text{Cluster\_ID} \rangle \longrightarrow \langle \text{Human\_Readable\_Label} \rangle$
\end{center}
These labels are then injected into downstream Sankey diagrams and dimensional scatter plots.

\paragraph{Consensus Alignment and Metrics (\texttt{alignment.py}, \texttt{metrics.py})}
Following the generation of macro-semantic clusters via BERTopic and lexical micro-clusters via GSDMM, these modules evaluate spatial cohesion across both outputs. The module \texttt{alignment.py} projects the probabilistic distribution of GSDMM onto the semantic boundaries defined by BERTopic using Jaccard similarity metrics:
\begin{equation}
    J(C_{\text{macro}}, C_{\text{micro}}) = \frac{|C_{\text{macro}} \cap C_{\text{micro}}|}{|C_{\text{macro}} \cup C_{\text{micro}}|}
\end{equation}

When an individual document exhibits an acute divergence between its semantic embedding neighborhood and its probabilistic lexical cluster assignment, the consensus logic degrades the document to an outlier state. This guarantees that only structurally coherent documents form the basis of the final thematic classifications.

\section{Experimental Validation}

\subsection{Stage 1: Baseline Replication and Field Completeness}

From the initial process into the 10,000 PubMed records, the preprocessing phase consolidated 15 duplicate entries, yielding a curated corpus of $N = 9{,}985$ unique articles. Table~\ref{tab:field_completeness} reports the final completeness rates across the 7 baseline fields.

Under the baseline prototype architecture, metadata enrichment relied on synchronous, single-threaded REST requests to Crossref, requiring upwards of two hours per attribute (around 12 hours across six fields) and incurring a 22.5\% loss in citation counts due to unhandled rate limits \cite{asano2026simplifying}. In contrast, our asynchronous multi-tier pipeline reconstructed all requested analytical attributes (\texttt{Author}, \texttt{Publication Year}, \texttt{Times Cited}, \texttt{Article References}, \texttt{Abstract}, and \texttt{Keywords}) in \textbf{less than 50 minutes}. While total runtime may scale up to roughly three hours depending on local workstation processing power, available bandwidth, and external API rate throttling, the asynchronous architecture reliably delivers a $>12\times$ throughput speedup over synchronous querying.

This performance acceleration stems from the balanced workload distribution across the API cascade. Tier~1 (OpenAlex) absorbed the bulk of candidate DOIs at high throughput ($\approx 6.6$ req/s), populating 64,582 discrete attribute lines in an initial sweep. Downstream tiers acted as targeted fallbacks for residual gaps: Tier~2 (Crossref) resolved 3,648 secondary records to recover missing author and publication year fields; Tier~3 (PubMed eUtils) and Tier~4 (Semantic Scholar) recovered 1,634 and 897 missing abstracts, respectively; and Tier~5 rescued legacy entries lacking persistent identifiers via fuzzy title matching. In total, 72.0\% ($n = 7{,}203$) of the corpus achieved complete coverage across all target attributes, 27.9\% ($n = 2{,}794$) gained substantial partial enrichment, and only three records remained unresolvable due to upstream indexing corruptions. \subsubsection{Demographic Equity Auditing and API Quota Resilience}
Evaluating author lists across the collection yielded a total volume of **27,708 unique authors** and 32,531 individual author mentions.

This volume illustrates a common operational hurdle in scientometrics: commercial name-to-gender classification services enforce strict request quotas (e.g., NamSor's free tier provides 2,500 credits per month), which is insufficient to evaluate a corpus of 27,000 authors.

The platform addressed this bottleneck through its multi-tiered demographic disambiguation cache. The persistent JSON registry and cold-start baseline lexicons resolved \textbf{28,599 author mentions} (87.9\%) completely offline without issuing a single external billable API call. The entire evaluation completed in 12.22 seconds.

Among authors with confirmed gender classifications (24,171 individuals; 87.2\% of the unique author pool), the audit revealed significant demographic disparities (Fig.~\ref{fig:gender_distribution}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Author Proportions:} The active workforce was heavily male-skewed, comprising 15,707 male authors (65.0\%) versus 8,464 female authors (35.0\%), with 3,537 authors remaining indeterminate due to single-initial formats or unisex phonetic classifications.
\item \textbf{Collaborative Team Structures:} Analysis across 9,504 multi-author teams revealed that male-majority collaborations accounted for over 64\% of production: 26.4\% all-male teams, 21.2\% male-majority teams, and 16.6\% single-male authors. Conversely, female-majority structures accounted for only 22.1\% (6.85\% all-female teams, 9.3\% female-majority teams, and 5.98\% single-female authors).
\item \textbf{Academic Scissors Effect:} Tracking author positional hierarchy across 9,901 evaluable articles revealed institutional attrition. While female researchers occupied **29.4\% of first-author positions** (mentee or lead researcher), their representation dropped to **25.3\% in last-author positions** (senior principal investigator or advisor), demonstrating a negative progression gap of **$-4.1$ percentage points**. In contrast, male representation expanded from 59.2\% (first author) to 63.4\% (last author), exhibiting a positive senior progression delta of **$+4.2$ percentage points**.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodological Baseline and Usability Disclaimer}
All evaluations reported in Stage~2 were executed under the software artifact's out-of-the-box, default configuration parameters (e.g., default Dirichlet priors, standard sentence transformer embeddings, and standard confidence cutoffs). As observed in recent domain-specific natural language investigations \cite{piceni2025musica}, extensive hyperparameter tuning, custom stopword dictionaries, and localized language models can improve cluster boundaries and classification granularity.

However, the primary engineering objective of this study was to demonstrate system resilience, stability, and automated error recovery under default operation, confirming that non-computational researchers and editorial boards can obtain actionable scientometric intelligence without manual parameter tuning or algorithmic intervention.























More formulas that can be useful:

The Hirsch $h$-index is calculated over sorted citation counts $c_1 \ge c_2 \ge \dots \ge c_N$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h_index}
h = \max \{ i \in \{1, \dots, N\} : c_i \ge i \},
\end{equation}
and Egghe's $g$-index, accounting for top-tier citation tail weight:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:g_index}
g = \max \left\{ i \in \{1, \dots, N\} : \sum_{j=1}^i c_j \ge i^2 \right\}.
\end{equation}

To capture citation inequality, the cumulative citation distribution is modeled via the Lorenz Curve:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lorenz_curve}
L(x_i) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^i c_{(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^n c_{(k)}}, \quad x_i = \frac{i}{n} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $c_{(1)} \le c_{(2)} \le \dots \le c_{(n)}$ are sorted ascendingly. The Gini Coefficient ($G$) is computed directly over ordered cumulative vectors:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gini_index}
G = \frac{n + 1 - 2 \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n S_i}{S_n} \right)}{n},
\end{equation}
in which $S_i = \sum_{k=1}^i c_{(k)}$ is the partial prefix sum and $S_n$ represents total citations.

Annual obsolescence and temporal decay are derived from moving averages:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:avg_citations}
\bar{C}_Y = 
\begin{cases} 
    \frac{C_Y}{P_Y}, & \text{if } P_Y > 0 \\ 
    \text{NaN}, & \text{if } P_Y = 0 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
protected via numeric exception trapping to maintain continuous curves.

\section{Discussion, Trade-offs, and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Modular Architecture and Extensibility}
\label{subsec:modular_architecture}
A defining characteristic of this platform is its highly decoupled, modular software architecture. Unlike monolithic bibliometric tools that require complete recompilation to introduce new visualizations, this system was engineered to scale seamlessly depending on user or customer requirements. Because the data ingestion and enrichment core operates independently of the reactive Streamlit frontend, developers can continuously integrate new features—such as custom LLM endpoints, specific indexing metrics, or alternative network layouts—as standalone "plugins" without disrupting the core pipeline. This ensures the platform can grow iteratively to meet specialized editorial demands or advanced scientometric research needs.

% ==============================================================================

\subsection{Client-Side Autonomy vs. Cloud Processing Trade-offs}
\label{subsec:tradeoffs}
The decision to enforce entirely local execution via Python and Streamlit provides an unparalleled advantage in data sovereignty, allowing independent researchers and editorial boards to analyze sensitive, unpublished manuscripts without exposing their intellectual property to proprietary cloud services. However, this architecture inherently bounds processing scale by local RAM and browser JavaScript limits. We empirically establish a comfortable operational ceiling of approximately 50,000 records, beyond which dynamic Plotly DOM rendering latency becomes perceptible.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Editorial Management}
\label{subsec:editorial_implications}
The platform transforms passive editorial boards into proactive stewards. By immediately flagging geographic concentration and monitoring endogeneity compliance, editors can circumvent unforeseen rejections from top-tier indexing services like Scopus and SciELO. Define abbreviations and acronyms the first time they are used in the text,
even after they have already been defined in the abstract. Abbreviations
such as IEEE, SI, ac, and dc do not have to be defined. Abbreviations that
incorporate periods should not have spaces: write ``C.N.R.S.,'' not ``C. N.
R. S.'' Do not use abbreviations in the title unless they are unavoidable
(for example, ``IEEE'' in the title of this article).

\subsection{Other Recommendations}
Use one space after periods and colons. Hyphenate complex modifiers:
``zero-field-cooled magnetization.'' Avoid dangling participles, such as,
``Using \eqref{eq}, the potential was calculated.'' [It is not clear who or what
used \eqref{eq}.] Write instead, ``The potential was calculated by using \eqref{eq},'' or
``Using \eqref{eq}, we calculated the potential.''

Use a zero before decimal points: ``0.25,'' not ``.25.'' Use
``cm$^{3}$,'' not ``cc.'' Indicate sample dimensions as ``0.1 cm
$\times $ 0.2 cm,'' not ``0.1 $\times $ 0.2 cm$^{2}$.'' The
abbreviation for ``seconds'' is ``s,'' not ``sec.'' Use
``Wb/m$^{2}$'' or ``webers per square meter,'' not
``webers/m$^{2}$.'' When expressing a range of values, write ``7 to
9'' or ``7--9,'' not ``7$\sim $9.''

A parenthetical statement at the end of a sentence is punctuated outside of
the closing parenthesis (like this). (A parenthetical sentence is punctuated
within the parentheses.) In American English, periods and commas are within
quotation marks, like ``this period.'' Other punctuation is ``outside''!
Avoid contractions; for example, write ``do not'' instead of ``don't.'' The
serial comma is preferred: ``A, B, and C'' instead of ``A, B and C.''

If you wish, you may write in the first person singular or plural and use
the active voice (``I observed that $\ldots$'' or ``We observed that $\ldots$''
instead of ``It was observed that $\ldots$''). Remember to check spelling. If
your native language is not English, please get a native English-speaking
colleague to carefully proofread your paper.

Try not to use too many typefaces in the same article. Also please remember that MathJax
can't handle really weird typefaces.

\subsection{Equations}
Number equations consecutively with equation numbers in parentheses flush
with the right margin, as in \eqref{eq}. To make your equations more
compact, you may use the solidus (~/~), the exp function, or appropriate
exponents. Use parentheses to avoid ambiguities in denominators. Punctuate
equations when they are part of a sentence, as in
\begin{equation}E=mc^2.\label{eq}\end{equation}

\subsection{LaTeX-Specific Advice}

Please use ``soft'' (e.g., \verb|\eqref{Eq}|) cross references instead
of ``hard'' references (e.g., \verb|(1)|). That will make it possible
to combine sections, add equations, or change the order of figures or
citations without having to go through the file line by line.

Please don't use the \verb|{eqnarray}| equation environment. Use
\verb|{align}| or \verb|{IEEEeqnarray}| instead. The \verb|{eqnarray}|
environment leaves unsightly spaces around relation symbols.

Please note that the \verb|{subequations}| environment in {\LaTeX}
will increment the main equation counter even when there are no
equation numbers displayed. If you forget that, you might write an
article in which the equation numbers skip from (17) to (20), causing
the copy editors to wonder if you've discovered a new method of
counting.

{\BibTeX} does not work by magic. It doesn't get the bibliographic
data from thin air but from .bib files. If you use {\BibTeX} to produce a
bibliography you must send the .bib files.

{\LaTeX} can't read your mind. If you assign the same label to a
subsubsection and a table, you might find that Table I has been cross
referenced as Table IV-B3.

{\LaTeX} does not have precognitive abilities. If you put a
\verb|\label| command before the command that updates the counter it's
supposed to be using, the label will pick up the last counter to be
cross referenced instead. In particular, a \verb|\label| command
should not go before the caption of a figure or a table.

Do not use \verb|\nonumber| inside the \verb|{array}| environment. It
will not stop equation numbers inside \verb|{array}| (there won't be
any anyway) and it might stop a wanted equation number in the
surrounding equation.

\section{Units}
Use either SI (MKS) or CGS as primary units. (SI units are strongly
encouraged.) English units may be used as secondary units (in parentheses).
This applies to papers in data storage. For example, write ``15
Gb/cm$^{2}$ (100 Gb/in$^{2})$.'' An exception is when
English units are used as identifiers in trade, such as ``3$^{1\!/\!2}$-in
disk drive.'' Avoid combining SI and CGS units, such as current in amperes
and magnetic field in oersteds. This often leads to confusion because
equations do not balance dimensionally. If you must use mixed units, clearly
state the units for each quantity in an equation.

The SI unit for magnetic field strength $H$ is A/m. However, if you wish to use
units of T, either refer to magnetic flux density $B$ or magnetic field
strength symbolized as $\mu _{0}H$. Use the center dot to separate
compound units, e.g., ``A$\cdot $m$^{2}$.''

\section{Some Common Mistakes}
The word ``data'' is plural, not singular. The subscript for the
permeability of vacuum $\mu _{0}$ is zero, not a lowercase letter
``o.'' The term for residual magnetization is ``remanence''; the adjective
is ``remanent''; do not write ``remnance'' or ``remnant.'' Use the word
``micrometer'' instead of ``micron.'' A graph within a graph is an
``inset,'' not an ``insert.'' The word ``alternatively'' is preferred to the
word ``alternately'' (unless you really mean something that alternates). Use
the word ``whereas'' instead of ``while'' (unless you are referring to
simultaneous events). Do not use the word ``essentially'' to mean
``approximately'' or ``effectively.'' Do not use the word ``issue'' as a
euphemism for ``problem.'' When compositions are not specified, separate
chemical symbols by en-dashes; for example, ``NiMn'' indicates the
intermetallic compound Ni$_{0.5}$Mn$_{0.5}$ whereas
``Ni--Mn'' indicates an alloy of some composition
Ni$_{x}$Mn$_{1-x}$.

Be aware of the different meanings of the homophones ``affect'' (usually a
verb) and ``effect'' (usually a noun), ``complement'' and ``compliment,''
``discreet'' and ``discrete,'' ``principal'' (e.g., ``principal
investigator'') and ``principle'' (e.g., ``principle of measurement''). Do
not confuse ``imply'' and ``infer.''

Prefixes such as ``non,'' ``sub,'' ``micro,'' ``multi,'' and ``ultra'' are
not independent words; they should be joined to the words they modify,
usually without a hyphen. There is no period after the ``et'' in the Latin
abbreviation ``\emph{et al.}'' (it is also italicized). The abbreviation ``i.e.,'' means
``that is,'' and the abbreviation ``e.g.,'' means ``for example'' (these
abbreviations are not italicized).

A general IEEE styleguide is available at \break
\underline{http://www.ieee.org/authortools}.

\subsection{Multipart figures}
Figures compiled of more than one sub-figure presented side-by-side, or
stacked. If a multipart figure is made up of multiple figure
types (one part is lineart, and another is grayscale or color) the figure
should meet the stricter guidelines.

\subsection{File Formats For Graphics}\label{formats}
Format and save your graphics using a suitable graphics processing program
that will allow you to create the images as PostScript (.PS), Encapsulated
PostScript (.EPS), Tagged Image File Format (.TIFF), Portable Document
Format (.PDF), Portable Network Graphics (.PNG), or Metapost (.MPS), sizes them, and adjusts
the resolution settings. When
submitting your final paper, your graphics should all be submitted
individually in one of these formats along with the manuscript.

\subsection{Sizing of Graphics}
Most charts, graphs, and tables are one column wide (3.5 inches/88
millimeters/21 picas) or page wide (7.16 inches/181 millimeters/43
picas). The maximum depth a graphic can be is 8.5 inches (216 millimeters/54
picas). When choosing the depth of a graphic, please allow space for a
caption. Figures can be sized between column and page widths if the author
chooses, however it is recommended that figures are not sized less than
column width unless when necessary.

There is currently one publication with column measurements that do not
coincide with those listed above. Proceedings of the IEEE has a column
measurement of 3.25 inches (82.5 millimeters/19.5 picas).

The final printed size of author photographs is exactly
1 inch wide by 1.25 inches tall (25.4 millimeters$\,\times\,$31.75 millimeters/6
picas$\,\times\,$7.5 picas). Author photos printed in editorials measure 1.59 inches
wide by 2 inches tall (40 millimeters$\,\times\,$50 millimeters/9.5 picas$\,\times\,$12
picas).

\subsection{Resolution }
The proper resolution of your figures will depend on the type of figure it
is as defined in the ``Types of Figures'' section. Author photographs,
color, and grayscale figures should be at least 300dpi. Line art, including
tables should be a minimum of 600dpi.

\subsection{Vector Art}
In order to preserve the figures' integrity across multiple computer
platforms, we accept files in the following formats: .EPS/.PDF/.PS. All
fonts must be embedded or text converted to outlines in order to achieve the
best-quality results.

\subsection{Color Space}
The term color space refers to the entire sum of colors that can be
represented within the said medium. For our purposes, the three main color
spaces are Grayscale, RGB (red/green/blue) and CMYK
(cyan/magenta/yellow/black). RGB is generally used with on-screen graphics,
whereas CMYK is used for printing purposes.

All color figures should be generated in RGB or CMYK color space. Grayscale
images should be submitted in Grayscale color space. Line art may be
provided in grayscale OR bitmap colorspace. Note that ``bitmap colorspace''
and ``bitmap file format'' are not the same thing. When bitmap color space
is selected, .TIF/.TIFF/.PNG are the recommended file formats.

\subsection{Accepted Fonts Within Figures}
When preparing your graphics IEEE suggests that you use of one of the
following Open Type fonts: Times New Roman, Helvetica, Arial, Cambria, and
Symbol. If you are supplying EPS, PS, or PDF files all fonts must be
embedded. Some fonts may only be native to your operating system; without
the fonts embedded, parts of the graphic may be distorted or missing.

A safe option when finalizing your figures is to strip out the fonts before
you save the files, creating ``outline'' type. This converts fonts to
artwork what will appear uniformly on any screen.

\subsection{Using Labels Within Figures}

\subsubsection{Figure Axis labels }
Figure axis labels are often a source of confusion. Use words rather than
symbols. As an example, write the quantity ``Magnetization,'' or
``Magnetization M,'' not just ``M.'' Put units in parentheses. Do not label
axes only with units. As in Fig. 1, for example, write ``Magnetization
(A/m)'' or ``Magnetization (A$\cdot$m$^{-1}$),'' not just ``A/m.'' Do not label axes with a ratio of quantities and
units. For example, write ``Temperature (K),'' not ``Temperature/K.''

Multipliers can be especially confusing. Write ``Magnetization (kA/m)'' or
``Magnetization (10$^{3}$ A/m).'' Do not write ``Magnetization
(A/m)$\,\times\,$1000'' because the reader would not know whether the top
axis label in Fig. 1 meant 16000 A/m or 0.016 A/m. Figure labels should be
legible, approximately 8 to 10 point type.

\subsubsection{Subfigure Labels in Multipart Figures and Tables}
Multipart figures should be combined and labeled before final submission.
Labels should appear centered below each subfigure in 8 point Times New
Roman font in the format of (a) (b) (c).

\subsection{File Naming}
Figures (line artwork or photographs) should be named starting with the
first 5 letters of the author's last name. The next characters in the
filename should be the number that represents the sequential
location of this image in your article. For example, in author
``Anderson's'' paper, the first three figures would be named ander1.tif,
ander2.tif, and ander3.ps.

Tables should contain only the body of the table (not the caption) and
should be named similarly to figures, except that `.t' is inserted
in-between the author's name and the table number. For example, author
Anderson's first three tables would be named ander.t1.tif, ander.t2.ps,
ander.t3.eps.

Author photographs should be named using the first five characters of the
pictured author's last name. For example, four author photographs for a
paper may be named: oppen.ps, moshc.tif, chen.eps, and duran.pdf.

If two authors or more have the same last name, their first initial(s) can
be substituted for the fifth, fourth, third$\ldots$ letters of their surname
until the degree where there is differentiation. For example, two authors
Michael and Monica Oppenheimer's photos would be named oppmi.tif, and
oppmo.eps.

When referencing your figures and tables within your paper, use the
abbreviation ``Fig.'' even at the beginning of a sentence. Figures should be numbered with Arabic Numerals.
Do not abbreviate ``Table.'' Tables should be numbered with Roman Numerals.

\section{\break Publication Principles}
Authors should consider the following points:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Technical papers submitted for publication must advance the state of knowledge and must cite relevant prior work.
\item The length of a submitted paper should be commensurate with the importance, or appropriate to the complexity, of the work. For example, an obvious extension of previously published work might not be appropriate for publication or might be adequately treated in just a few pages.
\item Authors must convince both peer reviewers and the editors of the scientific and technical merit of a paper; the standards of proof are higher when extraordinary or unexpected results are reported.
\item Because replication is required for scientific progress, papers submitted for publication must provide sufficient information to allow readers to perform similar experiments or calculations and
use the reported results. Although not everything need be disclosed, a paper
must contain new, useable, and fully described information. For example, a
specimen's chemical composition need not be reported if the main purpose of
a paper is to introduce a new measurement technique. Authors should expect
to be challenged by reviewers if the results are not supported by adequate
data and critical details.
\item Papers that describe ongoing work or announce the latest technical achievement, which are suitable for presentation at a professional conference, may not be appropriate for publication.
\end{enumerate}
