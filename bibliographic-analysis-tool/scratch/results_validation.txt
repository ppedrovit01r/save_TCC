\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

To establish an objective benchmark against Asano et al.~\cite{asano2026simplifying}, we utilized the corpus of 10,000 raw PubMed records retrieved under the query ``Artificial Intelligence''. To evaluate metadata repair capabilities under an unassisted cold-start scenario, the corpus was stripped of all existing metadata attributes prior to processing, preserving solely the primary accession keys: \texttt{Title}, \texttt{DOI}, and \texttt{PMID}.

The pipeline was evaluated in two stages: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Baseline Replication):} A direct comparison against the benchmark by Asano et al.~\cite{asano2026simplifying}, using the identical dataset (10,000 PubMed records under the query term ``Artificial Intelligence'') and constrained to the same 7 baseline fields.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Full-Scale Enrichment):} Scaling the architecture to the full 21-column schema to validate end-to-end features, multi-tier fallbacks, and throughput under heavy payload conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Stage 1: Baseline Replication and Field Completeness}
\label{subsec:stage1_results}

From the initial process into the 10,000 PubMed records, the preprocessing phase consolidated 15 duplicate entries, yielding a curated corpus of $N = 9{,}985$ unique articles. Table~\ref{tab:field_completeness} reports the final completeness rates across the 7 baseline fields.

Under the baseline prototype architecture, metadata enrichment relied on synchronous, single-threaded REST requests to Crossref, requiring upwards of two hours per attribute (around 12 hours across six fields) and incurring a 22.5\% loss in citation counts due to unhandled rate limits \cite{asano2026simplifying}. In contrast, our asynchronous multi-tier pipeline reconstructed all requested analytical attributes (\texttt{Author}, \texttt{Publication Year}, \texttt{Times Cited}, \texttt{Article References}, \texttt{Abstract}, and \texttt{Keywords}) in \textbf{less than 50 minutes}. While total runtime may scale up to roughly three hours depending on local workstation processing power, available bandwidth, and external API rate throttling, the asynchronous architecture reliably delivers a $>12\times$ throughput speedup over synchronous querying.

This performance acceleration stems from the balanced workload distribution across the API cascade. Tier~1 (OpenAlex) absorbed the bulk of candidate DOIs at high throughput ($\approx 6.6$ req/s), populating 64,582 discrete attribute lines in an initial sweep. Downstream tiers acted as targeted fallbacks for residual gaps: Tier~2 (Crossref) resolved 3,648 secondary records to recover missing author and publication year fields; Tier~3 (PubMed eUtils) and Tier~4 (Semantic Scholar) recovered 1,634 and 897 missing abstracts, respectively; and Tier~5 rescued legacy entries lacking persistent identifiers via fuzzy title matching. In total, 72.0\% ($n = 7{,}203$) of the corpus achieved complete coverage across all target attributes, 27.9\% ($n = 2{,}794$) gained substantial partial enrichment, and only three records remained unresolvable due to upstream indexing corruptions. Table~\ref{tab:field_completeness} summarizes the final completeness rates.

Under default parameterization (minimum cluster size $N_{\min} = 50$, synchronized topic count $k = 10$), an initial pass produced only four coarse clusters. This was caused by the presence of an acute `mega-cluster'' (Cluster 0) capturing 9,252 articles (93.2\% of the corpus). This phenomenon frequently impairs bibliometric topic modeling when a corpus is queried under a broad topical constraint: ubiquitous query terms (e.g., \textit{`artificial''}, \textit{`intelligence''}, \textit{`ai''}) dominate TF-IDF and embedding spaces, artificially collapsing distinct sub-specialties into a single uninformative super-cluster.

To resolve this without requiring manual corpus pruning, the platform's automated \textit{Mega-Cluster Breakdown Engine} dynamically intercepted the output (trigger condition: $>40\%$ corpus concentration). The module automatically froze ubiquitous lexical tokens exceeding 60\% intra-cluster document frequency and recursively partitioned the dense partition into seven granular sub-topics. As detailed in the execution log, this produced a final balanced taxonomy of \textbf{10 unified topics} with an average assignment confidence score of \textbf{0.88} and only 55 outlier documents (0.6\% assigned to noise class $-1$).

\textbf{Domain Coherence and Semantic Quality Assessment:} A qualitative audit confirms that the GenAI-synthesized topic labels exhibit high fidelity to the queried literature. Because the corpus was harvested from PubMed, the literature does not address theoretical computer science (e.g., compiler optimization or CPU architectures), but rather focuses on clinical and biomedical AI implementations. The GenAI naming module accurately identified fine-grained healthcare verticals:
\begin{itemize}

\subsubsection{Longitudinal Thematic Flow Across Five Eras}
To evaluate longitudinal paradigm shifts, the temporal modeling engine decomposed the 9,985 publications across five chronological windows: Era~~1 (1963--2021, baseline emergence), Era~~2 (2022--2023, deep learning expansion), Era~~3 (2024, post-pandemic consolidation), Era~~4 (2025, foundation model emergence), and Era~5 (2026--2027, multimodal integration).

Across these windows, Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) and Jaccard lexical tracking synthesized a directed acyclic graph containing **60 GenAI-labeled topics** (12 topics per temporal era), rendered as an alluvial Sankey diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:thematic_flow_sankey}).

Comparing the names generated across Thematic Flow against the static Thematic Stratification reveals consistent cross-module alignment: macro-domains identified in the static clustering (such as \textit{Drug Discovery}, \textit{Cardiovascular Imaging}, \textit{Digital Pathology}, \textit{Medical Education}, and \textit{Robotic Surgery}) consistently reappear as stable longitudinal threads in the flow mapping.

Crucially, the Sankey topology visualizes real-world paradigm evolutions across the literature:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Decision-Support Evolution:} Early foundations in \textit{Decision Support Systems} (1963--2021) split into \textit{Clinical Decision Making} (2022--2023), transitioning to \textit{Predictive Diagnostics} (2024), and ultimately integrating into \textit{Clinical Deep Learning} and \textit{Clinical Decision Support} in the modern era.
\item \textbf{Generative AI Emergence:} Classical text mining and informatics in early eras underwent rapid conceptual divergence following 2022, forming explicit lineages for \textit{Language Models} (2022--2023), \textit{Generative Language Models} (2024), \textit{Conversational AI} (2025), and culminating in \textit{AI Governance} and \textit{Scholarly Integrity} (2026--2027).
\item \textbf{Ethical and Regulatory Maturation:} The historical \textit{Medical Ethics} thread progressively branched into specialized operational sub-domains, including \textit{Data Ethics}, \textit{Health Equity}, \textit{Academic Integrity}, and ultimately institutionalized \textit{AI Governance}.

\subsubsection{Demographic Equity Auditing and API Quota Resilience}
Evaluating author lists across the collection yielded a total volume of **27,708 unique authors** and 32,531 individual author mentions.

This volume illustrates a common operational hurdle in scientometrics: commercial name-to-gender classification services enforce strict request quotas (e.g., NamSor's free tier provides 2,500 credits per month), which is insufficient to evaluate a corpus of 27,000 authors.

The platform addressed this bottleneck through its multi-tiered demographic disambiguation cache. The persistent JSON registry and cold-start baseline lexicons resolved \textbf{28,599 author mentions} (87.9\%) completely offline without issuing a single external billable API call. The entire evaluation completed in 12.22 seconds.

Among authors with confirmed gender classifications (24,171 individuals; 87.2\% of the unique author pool), the audit revealed significant demographic disparities (Fig.~\ref{fig:gender_distribution}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Author Proportions:} The active workforce was heavily male-skewed, comprising 15,707 male authors (65.0\%) versus 8,464 female authors (35.0\%), with 3,537 authors remaining indeterminate due to single-initial formats or unisex phonetic classifications.
\item \textbf{Collaborative Team Structures:} Analysis across 9,504 multi-author teams revealed that male-majority collaborations accounted for over 64\% of production: 26.4\% all-male teams, 21.2\% male-majority teams, and 16.6\% single-male authors. Conversely, female-majority structures accounted for only 22.1\% (6.85\% all-female teams, 9.3\% female-majority teams, and 5.98\% single-female authors).
\item \textbf{Academic Scissors Effect:} Tracking author positional hierarchy across 9,901 evaluable articles revealed institutional attrition. While female researchers occupied **29.4\% of first-author positions** (mentee or lead researcher), their representation dropped to **25.3\% in last-author positions** (senior principal investigator or advisor), demonstrating a negative progression gap of **$-4.1$ percentage points**. In contrast, male representation expanded from 59.2\% (first author) to 63.4\% (last author), exhibiting a positive senior progression delta of **$+4.2$ percentage points**.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodological Baseline and Usability Disclaimer}
All evaluations reported in Stage~2 were executed under the software artifact's out-of-the-box, default configuration parameters (e.g., default Dirichlet priors, standard sentence transformer embeddings, and standard confidence cutoffs). As observed in recent domain-specific natural language investigations \cite{piceni2025musica}, extensive hyperparameter tuning, custom stopword dictionaries, and localized language models can improve cluster boundaries and classification granularity.

However, the primary engineering objective of this study was to demonstrate system resilience, stability, and automated error recovery under default operation, confirming that non-computational researchers and editorial boards can obtain actionable scientometric intelligence without manual parameter tuning or algorithmic intervention.























More formulas that can be useful:

The Hirsch $h$-index is calculated over sorted citation counts $c_1 \ge c_2 \ge \dots \ge c_N$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h_index}
h = \max \{ i \in \{1, \dots, N\} : c_i \ge i \},
\end{equation}
and Egghe's $g$-index, accounting for top-tier citation tail weight:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:g_index}
g = \max \left\{ i \in \{1, \dots, N\} : \sum_{j=1}^i c_j \ge i^2 \right\}.
\end{equation}

To capture citation inequality, the cumulative citation distribution is modeled via the Lorenz Curve:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lorenz_curve}
L(x_i) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^i c_{(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^n c_{(k)}}, \quad x_i = \frac{i}{n} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $c_{(1)} \le c_{(2)} \le \dots \le c_{(n)}$ are sorted ascendingly. The Gini Coefficient ($G$) is computed directly over ordered cumulative vectors:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gini_index}
G = \frac{n + 1 - 2 \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n S_i}{S_n} \right)}{n},
\end{equation}
in which $S_i = \sum_{k=1}^i c_{(k)}$ is the partial prefix sum and $S_n$ represents total citations.

Annual obsolescence and temporal decay are derived from moving averages:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:avg_citations}
\bar{C}_Y = 
\begin{cases} 
    \frac{C_Y}{P_Y}, & \text{if } P_Y > 0 \\ 
    \text{NaN}, & \text{if } P_Y = 0 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
protected via numeric exception trapping to maintain continuous curves.

\section{Experimental Validation and Case Study}
\label{sec:validation}

To evaluate the operational resilience and analytical depth of the proposed platform, we conducted a rigorous stress test and a multidimensional case study using the historical archive of the RITA journal alongside a generalized 10,000-article benchmark corpus.

\subsection{Ingestion Robustness and Enrichment Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:exp_enrichment}
The core data ingestion and enrichment engine was subjected to a high-volume stress test using a baseline corpus of 10,000 records sourced from PubMed. Under the legacy synchronous paradigm \cite{asano2026simplifying}, resolving missing attributes via sequential REST calls required over 120 minutes and frequently crashed due to strict HTTP rate limiting. 

By implementing our five-tier asynchronous cascade, the proposed architecture processed the same 10,000-record corpus in just 26 minutes and 40 seconds—a computational speedup of over 4.5x. Notably, subsequent executions leveraging the local SQLite caching layer achieved processing times under 2 minutes (a $>15 speedup). Furthermore, the Tier 5 deterministic fuzzy-title search successfully recovered DOIs and metadata for 78.4\% of legacy articles completely lacking persistent identifiers. In total, 99.97\% (9,997/10,000) of the records were successfully enriched without triggering API blocks, effectively demonstrating the system's capacity to normalize sparse, unstructured exports.
